## An hour on a train is not free

A commute costs money and it costs time, and for most bank employees the saving on both was the largest change to their effective pay since the pandemic. Nobody granted it as a benefit. It arrived as an emergency measure, survived because the work got done, and hardened into an expectation. Now, according to a report this week, NatWest has told tens of thousands of staff to spend more time in the office. The union Unite called it a kick in the teeth — the union's words, not a finding of fact — but the phrase points at something real. Something of value is being taken back, and no one will record its value anywhere.

That is the part worth arguing about. Not whether offices are good. Whether a bank can change the economics of employment for 30,000 people without the change appearing in any of the elaborate machinery British banks maintain to disclose what they pay.

## Compensation that nobody wrote down

UK banks are among the most heavily disclosed employers in the country when it comes to pay. Base salary, variable pay, deferral periods, malus and clawback, pension contributions, the ratio of the chief executive's package to the median employee's — all of it is set out annually, because supervisors and shareholders decided over the past fifteen years that opacity around bank pay was itself a risk.

Working pattern escaped that entirely. It was never negotiated as a term, so it was never priced as one. When staff stopped commuting, the bank did not reduce salaries to reflect the gain, and nobody suggested it should. The flip side is that when the bank reverses the arrangement, it does not increase salaries either, and nobody will suggest that. Value moved one way without a debit and is now moving back without a credit.

This is not a complaint about employers being allowed to direct work. It is an observation that the single biggest shift in bank employment economics since 2020 sits outside the only system we built to make bank employment economics visible.

## Why a bank, specifically

For a retail employer this would be a human resources story. For a bank it is a governance one, for two reasons.

First, banks claim that location matters for control. The standard argument for attendance in regulated firms is supervision: trading and sales activity needs oversight, calls need recording, juniors need to learn judgement by sitting near people who have it, and culture is not transmitted over video. That argument may well be right. But it is a testable claim about risk management, and a board that believes it should be able to say where it applies and what evidence supports it. A bank that can tell its supervisor precisely how it monitors a mortgage adviser's phone calls should be able to say which control weakened when the adviser worked from a spare bedroom.

Second, banks have spent several years cutting cost-to-income ratios partly by shrinking property. If the occupancy decision is being made for reasons of culture, say so. If it is being made because expensive floors are sitting empty, say that instead. These are different decisions with different consequences, and the difference is exactly the kind of thing boards are supposed to explain.

## The strongest case for the bank

Here it is, put properly. A concession made during a public health emergency is not a permanent entitlement, and treating it as one would freeze every firm in the country into whatever arrangement happened to exist in 2021. Managers genuinely cannot see a struggling team over a video call. New entrants to banking learn the job by proximity, and a cohort trained entirely at home will be weaker supervisors in a decade. Collective bargaining over attendance would make routine operational decisions glacially slow. And employers who ask for more office time are not obliged to compensate for it any more than they compensate staff who choose to live further away.

Most of that is sound. But it argues for a stated rule applied where presence actually does something — training functions, supervised activity, control roles — not a uniform instruction across an entire workforce. And the last point proves too much. The reason nobody compensates for commuting is that commuting was always the baseline. Once a firm has run for years on a different baseline, moving it back is a change in terms in substance even where it is not one in law. Pretending otherwise is a legal position, not an honest accounting.

## What would settle it

A costing. Not a compensation payment — a number. How many staff are affected, by how many days, and what the bank estimates that is worth to them. Firms that can model operational risk to three decimal places can estimate a commuting burden. The reason it does not get published is not difficulty. It is that publishing it would invite the obvious follow-up question about who pays.

What makes this worth writing about is not that one bank changed an office policy. It is that the most significant movement in bank employee compensation this decade has happened twice, in both directions, entirely invisibly — and the disclosure regime built specifically to stop bank pay being invisible did not notice either time.
