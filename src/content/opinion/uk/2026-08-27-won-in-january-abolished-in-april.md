Britain's payments regulator spent two years trying to force down the fees banks charge each other every time you tap a card. Mastercard, Visa and Revolut took it to court to stop them. On 15 January 2026 it won — the Administrative Court upheld the power it had relied on.

Three months later the government announced it would be abolished. On 21 April the Treasury confirmed the Payment Systems Regulator will be folded into the Financial Conduct Authority, and on 19 May the Financial Services and Markets Bill had its first reading in the House of Lords.

## What actually moves

It is worth being precise, because the reflexive reading of this is deregulation and that is not what the Bill does.

The powers survive the move. On commencement the Financial Conduct Authority, the FCA, acquires the general direction power, the specific direction powers, the competition duty, the concurrent competition powers, and the mandatory reimbursement scheme for authorised push payment fraud — the rules that force banks to refund customers tricked into sending money to criminals. The Treasury has said it intends to keep the substance of the objectives, and the FCA remains responsible for competition, innovation and the interests of people who make payments.

The Financial Conduct Authority's own framing has been that this is an evolution rather than a revolution. On the law, that is fair.

## What does not move

An institution's willingness to use a power is not written into the power.

The Payment Systems Regulator had one subject. Payments were not part of its remit; payments were the whole of it. Every unit of attention it had went to card fees, access to payment systems, and fraud reimbursement. When the card networks pushed back hard enough to go to court, it defended its position, and won.

> A power that is one body's entire reason for existing becomes, inside a larger body, one item on a list.

The FCA is not a payments regulator that also does other things. It supervises conduct across the whole of financial services, and the same Bill that hands it payments also reforms the Financial Ombudsman Service, rewrites the consumer credit regime, changes bank ring-fencing and creates a new provisional licensing scheme. That is an enormous amount of institutional attention committed at once.

None of which means payments get neglected. It means payments now compete for attention, and they did not before.

## The case for consolidation

The argument for the merger is real and was made in good faith.

Two regulators covering overlapping ground produces duplication, and firms end up answering similar questions twice to bodies that do not always agree. The FCA and PSR had already been working closely on open banking, variable recurring payments and the payments vision work, so the practical distance between them was smaller than the org chart suggested. Consolidation removes a seam.

There is also a fair point about capability. A larger regulator has deeper legal resources, more data, and more ability to absorb a loss in court without existential consequences. The PSR's win in January was hard-fought against three well-funded litigants. The FCA fights that kind of case routinely.

And the Treasury has retained a check: it will be able to commission an independent review of the FCA's economic efficiency and effectiveness in payments. That is an unusual provision and it suggests the government anticipated exactly this objection.

## Why it still matters

Specialist regulators are inefficient by design, and that inefficiency is sometimes the point. A body that can only justify its existence through one subject will pursue that subject past the point a generalist would consider proportionate. Card fees are unglamorous, technical, and worth billions to a small number of very large firms with excellent lawyers. That is precisely the kind of fight a distracted regulator declines to pick.

The PSR has not gone yet. It keeps its statutory powers until the transfer completes and has published a work programme for 2026-27 covering card fees and fraud. The interesting period starts when it stops.
