## Two different questions

There are two kinds of supervisory question. One asks whether a particular product was sound. The other asks whether the firm that approved it can be trusted to approve the next forty. The second is far more useful, and far harder to turn into a headline.

According to the Financial Times, reported onward by Finextra, the Bank of England has instigated a review into how Revolut's product approval process works. That is the whole of what is publicly known. No scope, no timetable, no finding, no statement from the Bank or the firm, and — importantly — no allegation that anything has gone wrong. A review is not a conclusion. But the choice of subject is itself worth arguing about, because it tells you something about how supervision of fast-moving banks is going to work from here.

## What the gate is for

Every bank has an internal process for approving something new before it reaches a customer. A business line proposes it; risk, compliance, legal, finance and operations are asked whether it can be run safely; someone senior signs. The paperwork is deliberately dull. Its function is to put at least one person in the room whose pay does not depend on the launch date, and to force the firm to write down, in advance, what the product costs in capital, what it does to liquidity, what the complaints look like if it goes wrong, and how it would be withdrawn.

The gate is also where a bank's declared risk appetite stops being a slide and becomes a decision. Firms do not usually fail because one product was badly designed. They get into trouble because the mechanism that was meant to catch badly designed products had been quietly relegated to a formality — consulted late, overridden often, or staffed by people without the standing to say no.

## Why reviewing the process beats reviewing the products

A supervisor who examines one product learns about one product. A supervisor who examines the gate learns something about every product that will pass through it for the next several years. For any institution launching at speed, there are more launches in a quarter than a supervisory team can meaningfully inspect. Supervising the control rather than the output is the only approach that scales.

It is also the more honest instrument. Asking whether a firm's approval process works is not the same as asserting that a product failed, and the distinction matters for a firm whose entire commercial proposition is the pace at which it can put new things in front of customers. Speed is not a supervisory offence. Speed without a functioning brake is.

## The part that does not work

All of this happens in private. British supervision runs on firm-specific correspondence: letters out, responses in, remediation plans, follow-up. There are good reasons for that, and I am not asking for the file.

But consider what the current arrangement actually produces. Because the only public source is a press report, a named firm takes the reputational hit of being known to be under review, while the public gets none of the benefit. Outsiders cannot tell whether this is periodic supervisory housekeeping or a serious concern, and in the absence of that distinction they will assume the worse of the two. Meanwhile every other bank operating a thin approval gate carries on unexamined, because nothing has been published that would tell them what good looks like.

That is the wrong way round. The firm-specific judgement should be private; the generic standard should not be.

## The case against publishing anything

The strongest argument for the current practice is candour. Supervision works because firms tell their supervisor things they would never put in a press release, and they do that because they know the conversation stays in the room. Make the output public and the answers get drafted by lawyers. Add to that the market-sensitivity point: disclosing that a deposit-taking institution is under review, before anyone knows whether there is a problem, risks a reaction out of all proportion to the eventual finding. And reviews of this kind are, by design, routine — publishing each one would create a signal where none exists.

Each of those defends confidentiality of the firm-specific work. None of them defends silence about the standard. The Bank regularly sets out what it expects of firms in general terms without naming anyone, and it can describe what a credible new product approval process looks like — who must be consulted, who may override, what evidence of customer outcomes is required before launch rather than after — without disclosing a word about any individual institution.

## Routine or not

There is one more thing the Bank could do cheaply. When a supervisory review becomes public through the press rather than through the supervisor, the information asymmetry is at its worst: everyone knows that something is happening and nobody knows what kind of thing it is. A single sentence confirming whether an exercise is part of a scheduled programme or has been prompted by a specific concern would cost the Bank almost nothing and would remove most of the speculative premium that attaches to a leak-shaped story.

The question being asked here is the right one. It deserves an answer that more than two parties can read.
