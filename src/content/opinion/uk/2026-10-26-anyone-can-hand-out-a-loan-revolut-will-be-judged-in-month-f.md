Lending money is the easy half. Any firm with capital and a customer list can push cash out of the door. The test of a lender arrives in month fourteen, when a borrower loses shifts, or a relationship, or their health, and the direct debit bounces for the first time.

Revolut has added a personal loan for UK customers — a fixed sum, repaid in set monthly instalments over a fixed term, with nothing pledged as security. Unsecured credit, in other words. If the borrower stops paying there is no car to repossess and no house to charge. The lender's only recourse is the borrower's capacity and willingness to keep paying, plus whatever leverage a credit file and the county court provide. That product is a load-bearing wall of high street retail banking, which is plainly why anyone would want in.

## What scales and what doesn't

Underwriting scales. A decision engine that approves one application approves a hundred thousand at near-zero marginal cost, which is exactly why challengers start there and why the pitch to customers is always speed. Affordability checks scale too, in the narrow sense that income and expenditure data can be pulled and modelled automatically.

Collections do not scale. Forbearance — the practice of varying what a struggling borrower owes, by pausing payments, extending the term, freezing interest or writing off part of the balance — is a conversation. It requires a person with discretion, training and the authority to depart from the contract, and it requires that person to be reachable by someone who is frightened and embarrassed and quite possibly not in a state to type into a chat window. Every unit of lending growth creates a future claim on that function. The claim is small in good years and very large in bad ones.

This is the structural reason unsecured personal lending punishes new entrants. The revenue is booked immediately; the cost of servicing distress arrives two or three years later, concentrated in the minority of borrowers who struggle, and concentrated again in whichever recession happens to land on the book. A lender that grows fast into a benign credit environment looks excellent for exactly as long as the environment lasts.

## The month-fourteen test

There is a second asymmetry. A personal loan is not just a transaction; it writes on the borrower's permanent record. Repayment performance is reported to credit reference agencies and shapes what that person can borrow, rent and in some cases insure for years. Errors in that reporting propagate as efficiently as approvals do, and the burden of correcting them falls on the individual, who typically discovers the problem when they are refused something else.

So the questions worth asking on day one of a consumer credit launch are not about the interface. They are: how many people answer the phone when someone cannot pay; what they are permitted to agree without escalation; how quickly a missed payment turns into a human conversation rather than an automated reminder; and how the firm reports a borrower who has agreed a reduced payment plan. None of that is visible from the outside, for Revolut or for anyone else.

It is striking how little of the industry's attention goes here. The conference and whitepaper agenda running alongside this launch is real-time intelligence, AI-enabled exception handling, dispute workflows, cross-border corporate service gaps. All legitimate. None of it is the thing that determines whether a consumer lender is good or bad, which is a staffed, boring, expensive function that generates no growth story at all.

## The case for welcoming this

The strongest argument against my framing is that incumbency is not competence. The high street banks have closed branches at pace, routed distressed customers into call queues measured in tens of minutes, and have hardly covered themselves in glory on consumer credit conduct over the past two decades. A lender whose relationship with the customer runs through an app it controls end to end can, in principle, see trouble earlier than a bank that sees only a loan account: a salary credit that stops arriving, spending that shifts to essentials, a balance that no longer recovers before payday. Early detection is genuinely valuable, and a new entrant pricing keenly against a concentrated market is a straightforward consumer gain.

I accept all of that, and it does not dispose of the point. Detection is not remedy. Knowing on the fifth of the month that a customer is in trouble is only useful if it triggers someone with the authority to change the terms before the arrears compound. Data tells you who to call. It does not make the call, and it does not decide what to offer. The firms that handle distress well are the ones that have chosen to pay for that capacity in advance, when nothing is going wrong and the spending looks unnecessary.

## What would settle it

Nothing here is a prediction that this product will be run badly. It is an observation that nobody outside the firm can currently tell, and that the industry's habit is to find out in arrears — in both senses — three years after launch, via a complaints dataset and a supervisory review.
