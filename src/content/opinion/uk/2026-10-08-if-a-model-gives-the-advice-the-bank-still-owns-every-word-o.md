## A bad adviser damages one client book. A bad model damages all of them at once

That is the whole of the argument, and it is why a plan to replace most of a bank's advisers with software is a regulatory event rather than a human resources one.

The Financial Times, reported by Finextra, says HSBC intends to cut up to 70 per cent of its UK financial advisers as part of changes to its wealth management business driven by rising use of artificial intelligence, and one person describes the cuts as deep, wide and brutal. That is a press report, not a filing: the bank has published no terms, no description of what replaces the advisers, and no statement about the regulatory status of whatever it is. The absence is the interesting part.

## Advice is a legal category, not a job title

In Britain, telling an identified customer what to do with their own money — this pension, this amount, this product — is a regulated activity. It triggers a duty to establish that the recommendation suits that person, the Financial Conduct Authority's Consumer Duty to deliver good outcomes, a paper trail, and two backstops: the Financial Ombudsman Service, which can order compensation when a recommendation was unsuitable, and the Financial Services Compensation Scheme, which pays when the firm that gave it has gone.

Generic information carries none of that. The gap between the two is not a technicality. It is the difference between a customer with a claim and a customer with a grievance.

So when a firm removes most of the people who do the regulated thing, there are only two possibilities. The first is that the machine does the regulated thing instead. Nothing in British regulation says a personal recommendation must be made by a human; it says the firm is responsible for it. On that reading HSBC has not escaped a single duty. It has concentrated them into one system, which it must now supervise, test, document and stand behind — and the cost of doing that properly is not nothing.

The second possibility is that what replaces the advisers stops short of a recommendation: it narrows, explains, educates, nudges, and leaves the customer to press the button. That is cheaper precisely because the duties do not attach. It is also a transfer. The customer who would previously have had a suitability assessment and a route to the ombudsman now has an interface and a disclaimer.

## The file is the product

Every large remediation exercise British retail finance has run has turned on records: what was the customer told, when, and on what basis. A human adviser, whatever else is wrong with the model, generates that record as a by-product of doing the job. A generative system does not, unless it is built to.

Reconstructing a recommendation made two years ago means knowing which version of the model answered, what it was instructed to do, what the customer had entered, and what exact words came back. If any of that is overwritten on the next deployment, the firm cannot prove the recommendation was sound and the customer cannot prove it was not. Redress then becomes a negotiation about averages rather than an assessment of cases, which is worse for everyone except the lawyers.

## The case for doing it anyway

Here is the strongest version of the other side, and it is strong. Most British adults get no regulated advice at all, because at the price a qualified human must charge it is uneconomic to serve a modest pension pot. Advisers are scarce, ageing and expensive. A system that gives decent, consistent, documented guidance to a million people may do far more aggregate good than excellent advice delivered to forty thousand. Consistency is itself a virtue: software does not have a bad Friday, does not favour the products with the better conference, does not forget to ask about debts.

All true. But none of it argues for ambiguity about which category the service sits in. If the answer is that HSBC is extending genuine advice, with genuine liability, to people who could not previously afford it, that is a public good and the bank should say so loudly, because it is a selling point. The fact that firms in this position tend not to say so loudly is the tell.

## What would settle it

One sentence from the bank, and one paragraph from the regulator. Does the output constitute a personal recommendation? Is a customer who follows it and is harmed entitled to take the case to the ombudsman? And can the bank, on demand, produce what the system said and why, as it stood on the day it said it?

Those are answerable now, before anyone is made redundant. They will be very much harder to answer in 2031, when the first cohort of customers who followed an automated recommendation discover it did not suit them, and the advisers who might have remembered the conversation left five years earlier.
