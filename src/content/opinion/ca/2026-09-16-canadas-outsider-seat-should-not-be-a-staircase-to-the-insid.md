## The outside seat became an inside job

An outside voice at a central bank is only worth having if it can afford to be unpopular. The Bank of Canada has just started looking for one, and the reason the chair is empty is that the last person sitting in it was hired into the building permanently.

The notice itself is three lines of administration. The Bank's board of directors has launched a process to fill the external deputy governor position left vacant by the appointment of Nicolas Vincent as a full-time deputy governor. Nothing in that sentence is scandalous. But the mechanism sitting inside it deserves more attention than a recruitment announcement usually gets, because it quietly establishes what the external seat can lead to.

## What the seat is for

A central bank's senior ranks are mostly filled by people who have spent careers in central banking. That is not a criticism — monetary policy is technical, and institutional memory is worth something. But it produces a room where everyone was trained on the same models, by the same colleagues, and where the cost of being the lone objector is paid in the currency of internal reputation.

The external position is the designed answer to that. The Bank's own wording draws the distinction: the vacancy arose because its holder became a *full-time* deputy governor, which tells you the external role is something other than full-time. That is the point. The person is meant to have a life, an income and a professional standing that do not depend on the institution. They can say the forecast looks wrong, that the staff view is too comfortable, that the consensus formed too early — and go back to their other job on Friday.

Remove the independence of livelihood and you have not removed the seat. You have removed the reason for it.

## Why a promotion changes the job description

Once the external seat has visibly produced a permanent appointment, the next person to hold it takes on a second, unwritten role: candidate. Nobody has to be cynical for this to bite. It is ordinary human behaviour. People are more willing to press an uncomfortable point when pressing it costs them nothing, and less willing when the people they are pressing may later be deciding whether to hire them.

That is the whole argument, and it does not depend on anyone behaving badly. It depends only on the route existing and being known to exist. It now is.

There is a second problem stacked on the first. From outside the institution, nobody can verify what an external member actually argued. Unless the Bank publishes a record that identifies where members of the rate-setting council disagreed, the public has no way to check whether the outside voice was ever an outside voice. If you cannot observe the output, the only guarantee you have is the incentive structure around the person producing it — which is precisely what this vacancy has just weakened.

## The strongest case against me

Here is the best version of the other side, and it is not weak.

The promotion is evidence the arrangement worked. The Bank brought in an outsider, found the contribution valuable, and hired the person permanently. Treating that as a failure is perverse. Worse, a rule barring external members from permanent posts would force the institution to pass over a proven candidate for the sake of an abstraction, and would shrink the pool of people willing to take the external job in the first place — an ambitious economist may well accept a part-time public role partly because of where it might lead. Add that the board of directors, not the governor's office, runs the recruitment, which puts distance between the executive and the hiring decision.

All of that is fair. None of it survives contact with the specific fix being proposed.

I am not arguing the Bank should never hire a former external member permanently. I am arguing it should not be able to do so immediately. A cooling-off period — say, the length of one external term — costs the institution almost nothing. Talent does not evaporate in a couple of years, and if a candidate is strong enough to promote, they will still be strong enough later. What the waiting period buys is the one thing the seat cannot generate for itself: the certainty, visible to everyone including the appointee, that dissent carries no career price.

And the recruitment argument proves my point rather than rebutting it. If people are taking the external seat partly as an audition, they are not the candidates the seat was designed to attract. The Bank should want someone who would take the job and then decline the promotion.

The board's independence in hiring addresses the wrong stage. It governs who gets appointed. It does nothing about the incentive facing that person for every day of the term that follows.

## Fix it in the job description, not afterwards

This is a rare case where a governance problem can be solved in a paragraph of published text, before anyone is hurt by it, at no cost to anyone's reputation. The terms are not yet set in public. They should be.
