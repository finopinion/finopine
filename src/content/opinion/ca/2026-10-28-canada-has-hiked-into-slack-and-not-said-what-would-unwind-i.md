## A rate rise does not refine petrol

Raising the policy interest rate will not produce one more barrel of crude, nor one more litre of refining capacity. Yet on 28 October the Bank of Canada raised its target for the overnight rate by a quarter point to 2.5% — the first increase since July 2023 — and the reason given was, in substance, the price of fuel. Oil is dearer because of war in the Middle East. Petrol is dearer still because refinery margins are elevated, a phenomenon the Bank thought important enough to give its own section in the Monetary Policy Report.

The odd part is the rest of the Bank's description of Canada. The unemployment rate has been around 6½% for several months. A broad set of indicators continues to point to excess supply — slack, in plain terms: more capacity to produce than there is demand to absorb it. Into that, the Bank has tightened.

## What the Bank is actually buying

Tiff Macklem was straightforward about the logic. The increase is meant "to reduce the risk that elevated energy prices could spill over and become generalized inflation." That is not a claim that spillover has happened. It is insurance against second-round effects — the point at which a one-off rise in fuel costs gets written into wage demands and into the prices of things that have nothing to do with energy.

Insurance can be a sound purchase. But the Bank has published the premium — a quarter point of tightening on a slack economy — without publishing the terms. Its own projection has inflation above 3% through much of 2027 and easing after that precisely because energy and import cost pressures take time to pass through, with refinery margins assumed to decline gradually. That forecast contains its own answer. If the force pushing inflation up is expected to unwind on its own, higher rates are not fixing the first-round effect at all. The entire case rests on expectations. So the expectations evidence is the one thing that should have been put on the table, and it was not.

## The tightening that has already happened

There is a second problem. The Bank is adding to tightening someone else has already delivered. Its own statement records that global financial conditions have tightened since July, that advanced-economy sovereign bond yields are higher, and that equity and credit markets have been volatile on uncertainty about the artificial intelligence sector. The Report goes further and devotes a focus section to long-term sovereign yields reaching their highest levels in more than a decade across many advanced economies.

Canadian households renewing mortgages and firms raising term debt feel those long rates, not just the overnight rate. And the exchange rate offers no fresh excuse: the Canadian dollar weakened on US-dollar strength but has recovered to around 71 cents, exactly where the July projection assumed it. There is no new imported-inflation impulse from the currency. The financial conditions facing Canadian borrowers were tightening without any help from Ottawa's central bank.

## The case for hiking anyway

The strongest argument on the other side is memory. In 2021 and 2022 central banks met an energy and goods shock, judged it temporary, watched expectations move, and then had to tighten far harder and for far longer than an early move would have required. Monetary policy works with a lag; by the time de-anchoring is visible in the data it is already expensive to fix. This would be the second such shock in five years, and a second one lands on expectations that have not fully healed from the first.

Add to that the duration. Inflation above 3% through much of 2027 means a long stretch outside the target range whatever the cause, and ranges that are missed for years stop functioning as anchors. And the real economy looks able to take it: growth was 3.3% in the second quarter, around 2% in the third, with business investment rebounding and exports strengthening despite US tariffs.

That is a serious case, and the duration point is its best part. But the 2021 analogy breaks on the thing that matters. That episode ran through an economy with excess demand and a tight labour market. This one runs through an economy the Bank itself describes as having excess supply, with unemployment stuck near 6½%. The channel by which fuel costs become generalised inflation is bargaining power, and on the Bank's own reading there is not much of it about. Meanwhile "we cannot wait for the evidence" is an argument with no expiry date. It can justify a hike on any upside surprise, in any economy, forever.

## What would settle it

The Bank has claimed a risk. It should now name the instruments it reads that risk on. Not a general gesture at expectations being well anchored, but the specific series: which survey measures of expected inflation, which wage settlement data, which measure of how broadly price increases are spreading beyond energy. And it should say what readings on those series would mean the insurance is no longer needed.

This matters beyond one quarter point. A central bank that can hike on an unobserved risk and is never asked to show the risk has, in effect, freed itself from its own forecast.
