## A ban on yield does not abolish the yield

It relocates it. A stablecoin is a digital token that promises to be redeemable for one dollar, and that promise is made good by a pile of reserve assets held somewhere behind it. Those assets earn a return. If the issuer is forbidden from passing that return to the people holding the tokens, the return does not evaporate — it stays with the issuer. The prohibition is not a rule about whether yield exists. It is a rule about who gets it.

Canada's federal government has now put draft stablecoin regulations out for sixty days of public comment, and the reporting is clear on one point: the ban on yield payments survives. That single clause will shape the Canadian market more than anything else in the package, and it deserves to be argued about on its merits rather than waved through as a technical safeguard.

## The honest case for the ban

The strongest argument for prohibiting yield is not consumer protection. It is financial plumbing. A token that pays interest, is redeemable on demand at par, and carries no deposit insurance is a deposit in everything but name and legal protection. Let it pay competitively and two things follow. Funding migrates out of insured bank deposits, where it supports lending and sits inside a resolution regime, into an instrument with neither. And issuers, competing on the rate they advertise, acquire a reason to lengthen or de-quality the reserve to fund the payout — which is precisely the behaviour that turns a redemption queue into a run.

So the ban is coherent. What it is not is costless or neutral. It fixes the retail holder's return at zero while the issuer collects the whole of the reserve income, and it does so by law rather than by competition. Regulators should say that out loud, because a rule whose principal effect is a transfer of income from users to a licensed industry needs to earn its keep in other ways — in reserve quality, in redemption rights, in capital.

## The word that will do all the work

"Yield" is a narrow word, and narrow words leak. If the drafted prohibition binds the issuer and covers interest, a workable evasion is obvious to anyone who has watched payments: the issuer pays nothing, and an affiliated exchange, wallet or distributor pays a "reward" to users who hold the token on its platform. Call it cashback. Call it points. Call it a loyalty tier. The economics are identical — a payment conditional on holding a par-redeemable token — and the funding ultimately comes out of the same reserve income, routed through a related entity.

That is the only question about this clause that is genuinely open. Does the prohibition reach affiliates and third-party distributors? Does it reach non-cash benefits? Does it reach a rate paid by a platform that is contractually independent but commercially inseparable? If the answer to those is no, the ban will hold for about as long as it takes a legal team to read it, and the resulting market will be worse than one with permitted, disclosed, capital-backed yield, because the payments will exist but sit outside the frame.

## The case against this argument

The serious objection is jurisdictional. Canada can tell Canadian-regulated issuers what they may pay. It cannot stop a Canadian from holding a US dollar token issued abroad and earning a reward through an offshore platform. On that view a domestic yield ban does not protect anyone; it just makes the compliant domestic product the least attractive one on the shelf and pushes activity to venues no Canadian regulator supervises.

That objection is real but it argues for a different perimeter, not for abandoning the rule. The enforceable chokepoint is distribution to Canadians — the exchange, the wallet, the on-ramp converting Canadian dollars into tokens — not the issuer's charter. A rule that prohibits paying holding-contingent rewards to Canadian residents, whoever issues the token and wherever the platform is domiciled, is harder to draft and far harder to evade. A rule that binds only domestic issuers writes the offshore arbitrage into the statute.

## What the consultation should actually settle

Sixty days is enough time to answer three questions and no more. First, the definition: is a prohibited payment any benefit conditional on holding, from any related party, in any form? Second, the perimeter: does the rule attach to the issuer or to the act of distributing to Canadians? Third, the quid pro quo: if the reserve income belongs to the issuer by law, what share of it must be retained as loss-absorbing capital against the redemption promise, rather than distributed as profit?

That third question is the one nobody is asking. A licensed issuer with guaranteed float income and a statutory monopoly on it is a very good business. The public is entitled to ask what it is buying with that grant — and the answer should be a reserve and a capital buffer strong enough that par redemption holds on the worst day, not merely on an average one.
