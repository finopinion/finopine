## The whole point of a trustee is that it isn't you

Ripple said on Monday that the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency — the federal agency that charters and supervises national banks in the United States — has given it final, unconditional approval to operate a national trust bank. The charter is deliberately narrow. No deposits. No commercial lending. What it does permit, according to the company, is fiduciary custody of digital assets, management of the reserves supporting Ripple's dollar stablecoin RLUSD, and acting as collateral trustee for those reserves.

Read the last item again. A stablecoin is a token that promises to be worth a dollar because a pile of real assets sits behind it. The promise is only as good as the pile, and the pile is only as good as whoever is answerable for it. Corporate trusteeship exists precisely so that the person answerable for the assets is not the person who issued the promise. Here, on the firm's own account of what the charter allows, both roles can sit inside one corporate group.

## The word doing the heavy lifting

This approval came nearly a year after the Comptroller gave Ripple and four other crypto firms conditional approval. "Unconditional" means the pre-opening conditions have been met and the bank can open. It does not mean the bank operates free of supervisory constraint, and it certainly does not mean the thing most people hear when they hear the word bank. A national trust bank has no deposit insurance, because it takes no deposits. It is not a lender of last resort's customer in the ordinary way. The federal label buys examination and fiduciary standards; it does not buy a federal backstop for anybody holding the token.

That distinction matters more than usual right now, because the charter is being used commercially within days of being granted. Ripple announced a deepened arrangement with BNY — custody of tokenised assets, tokenised trade finance — and expanded work with Robinhood. The charter is not a compliance trophy. It is a sales asset, and the thing it sells is the idea that a federal regulator has inspected the arrangement and is satisfied.

## The conflict is structural, not personal

Nobody has alleged misconduct, and nothing here suggests any. The objection is about incentives under stress, which is the only condition in which trust arrangements are ever tested.

Imagine redemptions spike and one class of reserve asset becomes hard to sell at par. Somebody must decide the order in which assets are liquidated, whether to accept a haircut, whether to pause. A trustee owes those decisions to the beneficiaries. A subsidiary owes its existence to its parent. When the two are the same group, the fiduciary duty is real in law and thin in practice, because the board making the call is not independent of the party whose solvency is the question. Every other corner of structured finance treats this as obvious, which is why the trustee is almost always a third party with no equity interest in the outcome.

## The case for letting them do it

The strongest counter is Ripple's own, and it is a good one. Stuart Alderoty, the company's chief legal officer, framed the charter as the product of building inside the regulatory perimeter rather than around it. That is worth something. A supervised national trust bank keeps books the Comptroller can examine, submits to fiduciary standards, and can lose its charter. The realistic alternative for stablecoin reserves is administration by an entity in a jurisdiction where no examiner ever shows up. On that comparison, this is plainly an improvement.

There is a second answer: BNY has been the primary reserve custodian for RLUSD, so the assets are not sitting in a cupboard at the issuer. True — and commercially sensible. But custody arrangements are contracts, and contracts get renegotiated. Charters do not expire when a commercial relationship does. If the permission to act as trustee for one's own reserves is in the charter, it is available whether or not an outside custodian stays in the picture.

## Why execution, not approval, is the test

Wendy Cai-Lee of Piermont Bank made the sharpest observation of the week on this, at the same conference: getting a charter is one thing, getting a good rating at your annual safety and soundness exam is another. The staff who approve applications and the staff who examine banks are different people looking from different angles. Approval judges a plan. Examination judges what actually happened.

She said time will tell. The problem is that time cannot tell anyone outside the agency anything at all. Supervisory ratings are confidential. Approval orders and operating agreements, which are the one place where a condition on affiliate conflicts would appear, are not routinely put in front of the public in a form anyone reads. So a charter is announced as a seal of approval, the detail that would make it meaningful stays private, and the market is invited to infer the rest.

That is a bad bargain. The whole premise of chartering crypto firms rather than exiling them is that supervision produces trust. Trust that cannot be inspected is just branding with a federal crest on it.
