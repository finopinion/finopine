## The announcement that announces nothing

The Federal Reserve told the public something important about a Wisconsin bank holding company on Friday. It did not say what.

The Board announced on 2 October that it had executed a written agreement with Ontario Bancorporation, Inc., of Ontario, Wisconsin, dated 24 September. That is the entirety of the disclosure: a company name, a town, a document type, two dates and a link to an attachment. No description of the concern. No indication of whether this is about capital, lending standards, board oversight, record-keeping or something else entirely. The release is a template with the blanks filled in.

## What the words mean

Most American banks are owned by a parent company, and the Federal Reserve supervises those parents. A written agreement is the mildest formal tool the Fed has: a negotiated undertaking between the supervised firm and its regulator, carrying no fine and no finding that anyone broke the law. It sits above a quiet supervisory letter and below a cease-and-desist order. Supervisors use it when they want commitments in writing and enforceable, but do not think the situation warrants an order.

Precisely because it is mild, it is also the moment the shutters go up on everything else. A bank's examination rating — the composite score running from one to five that summarises capital, assets, management, earnings, liquidity and sensitivity to market risk — is confidential. So is every downgrade of it. So is every informal action taken before the formal one. The written agreement is the first thing the public is allowed to see, and for most small holding companies it is the only thing they will ever see.

## Why the format fails

That makes the wrapper matter more than the Fed seems to think. A depositor in a small Wisconsin town who learns that their bank's parent is under a written agreement has been given a fact with no scale attached. Is this a bookkeeping dispute or the beginning of the end? The announcement is silent, and silence in banking is read pessimistically by the few people who notice and ignored entirely by everyone else.

The attachment may well say more — these documents commonly set out what the parties have agreed. But the burden of downloading and construing a legal instrument falls hardest on exactly the people with the least capacity to carry it. Sophisticated counterparties already know what is going on; they have correspondent relationships and credit analysts. The local small business with its operating account does not.

## The case that the silence is working

There is a serious argument on the other side, and it deserves stating properly. Banking runs on confidence, and a supervisor's job is partly to fix problems without triggering the loss of confidence that would turn a fixable problem into a failure. Publishing "liquidity concerns" alongside a bank's name could do precisely the damage the agreement exists to prevent. The negotiated bargain is that the firm commits to remediation and the regulator does not broadcast the diagnosis. On this view, the thin press release is not an oversight; it is the whole point.

I do not buy it, for two reasons.

First, the disclosure is already partial, and partial disclosure is the worst available setting. Naming the firm does whatever alarming it is going to do. What the current format withholds is not the alarm but the proportion — the information that would let a reader conclude "this is a governance matter, not a solvency one" and go back to their day. A one-word subject category is not a rating and does not reveal an examiner's judgement of severity.

Second, the confidential route has a visible failure record. Tioga-Franklin Savings Bank, a single-branch Philadelphia lender, failed on 21 August. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation's inspector general reported last week that the FDIC had identified concerns there since 2022 and taken both formal and informal action; examiners cut the bank to a composite four in July 2023 over asset quality, earnings and management oversight, and dropped its liquidity rating to the worst grade in March 2024. Roughly three years of escalating private supervision did not save it. The insurance fund absorbed an estimated $5.4 million on a bank with under $67 million of assets, and the public account of what went wrong arrived two and a half months after the doors closed.

That is not an argument that disclosure would have saved Tioga-Franklin. It is an argument that the confidentiality bargain is being priced as though it always works, when the record shows it sometimes simply delays the bill.

## The small, cheap fix

None of this requires publishing examination ratings, examiner narratives or the supervisory correspondence that genuinely does need protection. It requires the Board to write one more sentence. Something of the order of: "The agreement addresses board oversight, capital planning and dividend restrictions at the holding company." That sentence already exists, in substance, in the document the Fed is attaching. Keeping it out of the announcement is a choice about format, not about confidentiality — and it is the wrong one.
