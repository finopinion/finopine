## What the card networks actually sell

Visa and Mastercard barely own a wire. The money in a card payment moves through banks. What the networks move is a message, and what they sell alongside it is a set of promises about the day the payment goes wrong: the merchant gets paid, the buyer can reverse the charge when the parcel never shows up, fraud losses land on a party named in advance, and one acceptance contract works in every country the brand operates in. That bundle, not the messaging, is the moat.

Five of Europe's domestic payment schemes — Bancomat, Bizum, Wero, Sibs-MB WAY and Vipps MobilePay — have now agreed to build a cross-continent network aimed squarely at the two American incumbents. The engineering is tractable. These are modern, account-based systems with national adoption their owners earned the hard way. What has not been shown is whether anyone has written the promises down.

## Reach is the cheap half

Payments is a two-sided market, and the famous difficulty is that consumers go where merchants are while merchants go where consumers are. Linking five national schemes solves the consumer side almost by decree: millions of people who already have an app can suddenly reach each other across borders. That is a real achievement for person-to-person transfers and for the political argument that Europe should not clear every retail payment through firms headquartered in California and New York.

It does not put a single new acceptance point in a shop or a checkout page. A scheme that works beautifully between two phones in different countries and nowhere in between is a contact list, not a card network. The question for anyone in Purchase or Foster City reading this is not whether Europeans can now pay each other. It is whether a merchant in Hamburg will be offered one agreement that works for a Spanish customer, a Portuguese one and a Norwegian one, with the same rules for each.

## Finality is a feature and a problem

Instant account-to-account payments are built to be irrevocable within seconds. That is the point: it removes settlement risk and it is why the rails are cheap. It is also precisely what a buyer does not want when they have paid a stranger for something that never arrives. The card chargeback exists because consumers will not hand money to unfamiliar merchants without a way to get it back, and someone — the acquiring bank, ultimately the merchant — carries that risk.

So the alliance faces a tension it cannot engineer away. Offer no dispute rights and the network stays a transfer tool for people who already trust each other. Offer dispute rights and somebody must fund them, which means a cross-scheme fee charged to the merchant side. A fee charged to the merchant side to fund consumer protection is interchange by another name, and interchange is the thing European policy spent a decade capping. The pitch "cheaper than cards" and the pitch "as safe as cards" pull in opposite directions, and the rulebook is where that fight has to be settled.

## Then there is credit

Cards are also a lending product. A meaningful slice of card spending is deferred, revolving or otherwise funded by the issuer rather than the balance in a current account. A push payment from a bank account is debit by construction. Nothing stops a participating bank bolting credit onto a wallet, but that is a separate product with separate underwriting, separate regulation in each country, and no guarantee of a common experience across five schemes.

## Five owners, one committee

Visa and Mastercard write one rulebook and enforce it on thousands of member banks, with the power to fine them. This alliance is five scheme owners, each answering to national banking communities, national supervisors and incumbent fee arrangements, plus the euro-area wallet project. Every meaningful rule — refund windows, fraud liability allocation, who eats currency conversion between the euro and the Nordic currencies, what happens when one scheme's bank is slow to respond — is a negotiation among parties who can each walk away. Committees produce interoperability. They are much worse at producing enforcement.

## The case against this argument

The strongest counter is that nobody needs to kill the card networks to win. A credible domestic substitute disciplines incumbent pricing even at modest share; merchants acquire a walk-away threat they have never had; and the political economy in Europe is unusually favourable, with instant payments mandated and strategic autonomy fashionable. On that view the alliance is already doing its job by existing.

I accept the mechanism and reject the timing. Price discipline requires a substitute a merchant could plausibly adopt next quarter, which means acceptance, dispute rules and a predictable cost, all published. Incumbents respond to that. They do not respond to a shared logo and a statement of intent, and the European payments landscape already contains more than one initiative that got as far as the announcement.

## What would settle it

One document would settle it: a common rulebook binding all five schemes, covering who refunds a buyer whose goods never arrive, who bears fraud losses, what a merchant pays, and when in-store and online acceptance begins outside each scheme's home market. Everything else is a question of will.
