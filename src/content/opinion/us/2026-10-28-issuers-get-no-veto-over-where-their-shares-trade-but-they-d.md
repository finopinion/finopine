## A company does not own its own shareholder list

Once a company has sold its stock, what happens to that stock is the buyer's business. Secondary trading is a market between owners; the issuer is a bystander to it. That is not a technicality, it is the foundation of the thing. A firm that could choose its trading venues would choose the ones with the least price discovery, the least short selling and the fewest inconvenient questions.

So when a group of very large companies registers an objection to their shares appearing on a planned tokenised platform, the first instinct should be scepticism. There is no general right to be absent from a market, and granting one to JPMorgan Chase and Goldman Sachs — two of the largest intermediaries in the market as it currently stands — would be a particularly odd place to start.

## The question hiding inside the complaint

And yet the objection points at something nobody has settled. The word "tokenised" does not describe a single product. It describes at least three, and they are not close relatives.

A token can be the share itself, with the distributed ledger operating as the legal record of ownership. It can be a receipt: somebody buys and holds the real share, and issues a digital claim on it. Or it can be a contract whose value merely references the share price, with no underlying stock anywhere in the structure.

All three can be labelled with the same ticker and the same corporate name. The rights attached to them are wildly different. Does the holder vote? Does the holder get the dividend, and on what day — the company's payment date, or whenever the wrapper's operator gets round to it? What happens in a merger, a tender offer, a stock split, a rights issue? And if the platform or the custodian fails, does the holder own a share, or own a place in a queue of creditors?

A company whose name is on the front of the instrument has a legitimate interest in the answer, because it is the company that fields the call when a token holder asks why no proxy card arrived. That is a reasonable thing to demand. Removal from the venue is not.

## Who is building it matters

This is not a crypto curiosity operating at the edge of the regulated world. Intercontinental Exchange is the parent of the New York Stock Exchange. The venue is being built with OKX, a crypto exchange. That pairing is precisely what makes the design question urgent rather than academic: this is the established listing infrastructure lending its name and its operational credibility to a wrapper business, with a crypto distribution partner attached.

It also removes the usual excuse. When offshore platforms have offered tokenised versions of American stocks, the standard response from the regulated end of the market has been that this is someone else's jurisdiction and someone else's problem. It is nobody else's problem now. If ICE can build it, ICE can document it.

## The strongest case against me

The obvious reading is protectionism. A tokenised venue trading around the clock and settling in seconds routes around the clearing, custody and execution businesses the objecting banks run. Of course they object. Their complaint about shareholder rights is a convenient dress for a commercial interest.

There is something in that, but two things cut against it. Microsoft is not a broker-dealer and has no plumbing to defend; its objection cannot be explained that way. And more importantly, a self-interested question can still be a good question. The test is whether disclosure answers it. If the tokens are clean — real shares, real custody, votes and dividends passed through on the issuer's timetable, bankruptcy-remote holdings — then publishing the terms destroys the objection at no cost. The only reason to resist publishing is that the answers are untidy.

The second defence is more serious: tokenisation offers genuine gains. Settlement in seconds rather than days frees up collateral. Fractional units widen access. Continuous trading suits a world where the holders are not all in one time zone. All true, and all a reason to get the disclosure right rather than to let the product arrive undefined. Innovation that depends on nobody reading the terms is not innovation.

## What would actually settle this

Not a vote by issuers. A document. One page per security, in a standard format, published before trading begins: legal form of the token, location and identity of the custodian, voting pass-through and how it is exercised, dividend mechanics and timing, treatment of each category of corporate action, and the holder's position if the operator fails.

If that page exists and reads well, the objections collapse into noise. If it does not exist, then the companies raising their hands have done the market a service, even if the remedy they want is the wrong one.
