## The promise is the product

Every stablecoin rests on a sentence, not a pile of assets: hand the token back, get a dollar. The Federal Reserve has now written the pile-of-assets part in considerable detail and left the sentence for later.

Last Thursday the Board put out two proposals for public comment, the first serious instalment of the framework the GENIUS Act requires federal regulators to build for payment stablecoin issuers. The first proposal would require Board-supervised issuers to fully back their stablecoins with certain permissible reserve assets — short-term Treasury bills and other high-quality liquid assets — and would impose standardised capital requirements addressing certain credit and operational risks, plus risk management standards. It would also set rules for Board-supervised firms that safekeep those reserves, and clarify which stablecoin activities supervised banks may conduct at all. The second proposal is procedural: a tailored application process for banks that want to issue, with rules for appeals, hearings and final determinations.

That is a real body of work, and on reserves it is the right instinct. But read Governor Michael Barr's statement issued alongside it. He supports the rulemaking. He is encouraged by the reserve asset limitations and by transparent, standardised capital. Then comes the line that should shape every comment letter: it will be important that universal redemption rights are clear in the final rule to support public confidence.

## Two words doing all the lifting

"Final" and "universal."

"Final" is an admission. A governor voting to put a proposal out does not ask for something to be made clear in the final rule if it is already clear in the proposed one. The redemption question is live, and it is live at the stage where the Fed is simultaneously opening a queue for bank applicants.

"Universal" is the substantive point. Redemption at par can mean two very different regimes. In one, any holder of the token can present it to the issuer and receive a dollar within a defined period. In the other, only the issuer's direct, onboarded, minimum-size counterparties can do that, and everyone else exits by selling to a market maker at whatever the market will bear. Both regimes can be described in a marketing document as "redeemable at par." Only the first is a right. The second is an arbitrage channel that usually works, and the qualifier "usually" is the entire regulatory question.

Barr's own description of stress tells you why. Stablecoins will only be stable, he says, if they can be reliably and promptly redeemed at par in a range of conditions — including market stress, when pressure can be put on the value of even otherwise liquid government debt, and during episodes of strain on the individual issuer or its related entities. Each clause names a different failure. Liquid does not mean price-insensitive when everyone sells the same bills on the same morning. And "related entities" is the affiliate problem: an issuer sitting inside a group that also runs a trading venue, a lender or a payments business, where the reserve pool is pristine but the phones stop being answered.

## The word "certain" is where the discretion lives

Notice how the Board's own summary is hedged. Certain permissible reserve assets. Certain credit and operational risks. Barr asks, explicitly, for public input on whether the rule adequately addresses interest rate and foreign currency risks — risks that are not in the list of things the proposed capital requirement is scoped to cover. A token promising one dollar, backed by instruments with a duration and possibly a currency other than the dollar, has a market risk the capital charge as described does not obviously price.

This is not a complaint that the Fed asked questions. It is a complaint about which questions were left open while which parts were drafted. Reserve eligibility and capital arithmetic are written down. The consumer's claim is a question for the record.

## The case for doing it this way

The strongest defence is procedural and it is a good one. A proposed rule is precisely the place to flag unresolved design choices, and a governor publishing his doubts is the system working in daylight rather than a dissent leaking later. The statutory deadline for these regulations has already passed; an honest proposal with hard questions attached beats another quarter of silence. And redemption mechanics are genuinely hard — a rigid same-day obligation on every holder, however small, could force fire sales that break the peg the rule is trying to protect.

All true. But comment periods are not neutral instruments. The parties with the resources to file detailed letters on redemption scope are issuers, banks and the intermediaries who would be the privileged redeemers. The holder of forty dollars of a token on a phone files nothing. When the drafted parts of a rule favour the balance sheet and the open parts concern the customer's claim, the process has a thumb on the scale — not through bad faith, but through who shows up.

And the sequencing is the real hazard. The second proposal would let banks start applying. Approvals granted against a framework in which universal redemption is still an open question become facts on the ground that the final rule has to accommodate.
