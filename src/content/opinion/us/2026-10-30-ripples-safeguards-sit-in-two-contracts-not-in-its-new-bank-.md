## The charter is the least interesting thing that happened

Three things landed in the same announcement: a federal trust bank charter, a partnership with BNY, and a partnership with Robinhood. Only one of them is a supervisory act. The other two are commercial deals — and they are the two that do the actual risk reduction.

A custodian the size of BNY holding reserve assets is a genuine improvement on an issuer holding its own reserves at an affiliate. Distribution through a regulated retail brokerage is a genuine improvement on distribution through an offshore exchange nobody can serve process on. Both are good. Neither is a condition of the licence as far as anyone outside the Comptroller's office can tell, because the approval order and the conditions attached to it are not what the press release is about.

So the structure the public is being asked to take comfort from is: a federal charter, plus two contracts. Contracts get renegotiated. Custody mandates move. Distribution agreements lapse. The charter is the only part that stays, and the charter is the part we know least about.

## What a trust charter was built to do

A national trust bank is not a deposit-taking bank. It is a federally chartered institution that holds and administers assets belonging to other people — securities custody, corporate trust, fiduciary administration — and earns fees for doing it. The supervisory model is built around that: are you keeping other people's property separate from your own, are your records right, are you honouring the terms of the instruments you administer.

The attraction to a digital asset firm is not fiduciary prestige. It is preemption. One federal licence displaces the patchwork of state money transmitter registrations that currently governs anyone moving dollars electronically. Galaxy Digital's chief executive said as much when his firm applied for the same charter this week: custody, trading, staking and fiduciary advice, all under a single federal framework. That is an honest statement of the appeal, and it tells you the charter is being used as a passport rather than as a description of the business.

The mismatch is this. The risk in fiduciary custody is operational — bad records, commingled assets, sloppy administration. The risk in issuing a dollar-denominated token that circulates as a payment instrument is a run: everyone asks for their dollar back on the same afternoon. Those are not the same hazard, and a supervisory programme calibrated for the first does not automatically cover the second.

## Distribution changes the arithmetic

The Robinhood leg is what turns this from a wholesale arrangement into a retail one. A token sitting on institutional desks and a token sitting in the app where millions of people check their portfolio on the train are different products with the same code. The second has a redemption profile driven by sentiment, headlines and the behaviour of people who did not read the terms.

It also creates a labelling problem the word "bank" makes worse, not better. A customer who sees that the issuer is a federally chartered national bank, inside an app they already associate with regulated brokerage, is being given three reassuring signals at once. None of those signals is deposit insurance. Trust charters are chosen precisely because they are not deposit-taking charters. That is a legitimate structure, but it needs to be stated at the point of sale in words a person would use, not in a risk disclosure appended to a terms-of-service update.

## The case for leaving it alone

The honest counter-argument is that this is all strictly better than the status quo. Before, a large dollar-token issuer sat outside federal bank supervision entirely, reachable only through a mosaic of state regimes with no consolidated view of the whole. Now the Comptroller has an examination relationship, a direct line to management, and the ability to attach conditions to a charter it has granted and can act against. Supervision that exists beats supervision that is theoretically available in thirty-eight states.

That is right, and it is why the approval is defensible. But it answers a question nobody is asking. The issue is not whether federal supervision is better than none; it is whether this particular federal supervision is specified for this particular risk, or whether everyone is quietly relying on a custody contract and an implicit assumption that a firm with a charter will behave as if it had one.

## The queue is the reason to settle it now

Ripple is not the last application. Galaxy is already in. The decision the Comptroller makes about what a trust charter requires of a money-issuer is, functionally, the rulebook for a new category of American financial institution, and it is being written one approval order at a time, in documents that attract far less attention than the partnership announcements they arrive alongside.

That is the wrong order. The template should be published before the queue clears, not reconstructed afterwards from a dozen inconsistent orders — because by then every issuer will have been chartered against whatever standard happened to apply the week its application was read.
