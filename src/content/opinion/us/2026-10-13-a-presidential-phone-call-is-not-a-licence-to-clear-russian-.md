## Nobody loads a cargo on a post

The first people who have to act on a deal bringing Russian diesel to America are not oil traders. They are compliance officers at banks and insurers — and right now they cannot act at all.

President Trump says he had a "highly successful" call with Vladimir Putin in which they agreed Moscow would immediately supply more than 300,000 metric tonnes of diesel to American and global markets. Last month the same president signed a sweeping sanctions law aimed at clamping down on Russia's energy revenues. Those two facts have to be reconciled by somebody. The somebody is not the president, and the reconciliation is not a press statement. It is a document.

## Sanctions are a banking rule before they are a foreign policy

It is tempting to read sanctions as something governments do to other governments. In practice they are a set of instructions to private intermediaries. A barrel of diesel physically moves because a shipowner accepts the charter, an insurer writes the cover, a port accepts the vessel, a buyer's bank issues the credit and a correspondent bank somewhere clears the dollars. Every one of those steps is a firm deciding whether it is willing to carry a legal liability.

Sanctions bite by making that answer no. They do not need prosecutions to work; they need the fear of one. So the binding constraint on this deal is not Russian refinery capacity or tanker availability. It is whether a compliance function will sign off, and a compliance function cannot sign off on an announcement.

## The question is what you show an examiner in 2031

The relevant internal question at a bank is never "does the administration want this?" It is "what do I produce when a regulator reviews this payment years from now?" Sanctions enforcement has a long tail. Investigations open after the fact, under different political management, and they are resolved by looking at what written authority the firm relied on when it moved the money.

The risk is wildly asymmetric. Clearing one diesel payment earns a fee measured in basis points. Getting it wrong earns a penalty, a consent order and years of supervisory attention. No rational chief compliance officer takes that trade on the strength of a social media post, however senior the account. Which means that either the cargoes do not move, or someone moves them while self-authorising — and that second outcome is much worse than the first.

## The premium that does the actual work

The deeper cost is to the instrument itself. Most of what sanctions achieve comes from over-compliance: firms declining business that is probably lawful because the margin of error is not worth it. That caution is not written in any statute. It rests entirely on the belief that the rules will still be the rules next year.

Show that an energy sanctions regime can be suspended by telephone and that belief erodes in both directions. Firms become less cautious today, and they discount the next round of designations before it is announced — against Russia or anyone else. Washington would be spending the credibility of a weapon it uses constantly in order to buy a few cargoes of fuel.

And a few cargoes is what this is. Analysts quoted in the reporting already doubt the deal will do much for diesel prices sitting near record highs. Diesel is near-record-priced because refining and distribution are tight across a continental fuel system; a single negotiated parcel is not a structural answer to that.

## Allies are not following, and that costs money too

Australia's energy minister, Chris Bowen, said his country "doesn't need or want Russian fuel" and that arrangements are in place with trading partners to keep it out. That is one ally, but it signals the shape of the problem: a global bank with branches on both sides now has to run two rulebooks over the same barrel, and reconcile a US position that may be permissive with partner regimes that are not. Divergence between sanctions regimes is one of the most expensive things in banking operations, and it is paid for in screening systems and headcount, not in headlines.

## The strongest case for the other side

Licensed trade inside a sanctions regime is completely normal. Food, medicine, wind-down periods, humanitarian channels — carve-outs are the rule, not the exception. And relief is the entire point of leverage: a president who cannot offer to lift anything has nothing to negotiate with. On that reading, this is sanctions policy working exactly as designed, with the paperwork simply lagging the diplomacy.

That argument is right about carve-outs and wrong about sequencing. Leverage requires that relief be granted *against* something, documented, time-limited and revocable. Here the supply was announced first and the legal authority, if it exists, has not been shown. Relief handed over because the sanctioning country would like cheaper diesel is not leverage at all. It is a purchase, and the seller now knows the price.
