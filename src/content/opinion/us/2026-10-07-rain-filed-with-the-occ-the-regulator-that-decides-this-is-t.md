A national trust bank cannot take an insured deposit. That is not a defect in the application Rain filed this week with the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency — the agency that licenses national banks in the United States — it is the entire design. A trust charter is permission to hold and administer other people's assets under fiduciary duty, with no government insurance fund standing behind it. Rain, which describes itself as enterprise-grade infrastructure for stablecoin payments, wants one, headquartered in New York. Modern Treasury filed in the same stretch of days. Anchorage Digital, which already holds this kind of charter, spent the week buying a payouts company to bolt settlement onto issuance and custody.

So the pattern is clear enough. What is not clear is who actually decides whether any of it works.

## The charter is the easy half

A charter buys three things: a federal supervisor, a federal name, and a legal basis for holding customer assets in your own institution rather than renting space inside somebody else's. For a payments firm that has spent its life as a vendor sitting behind partner banks, the third is the prize. It removes a layer of counterparty, a layer of fee, and a layer of ambiguity about whose money is whose when something goes wrong.

What it does not buy is an account at the Federal Reserve. The Comptroller has no power to grant one. Reserve balances, access to the settlement rails, the ability to hold dollars as a direct claim on the central bank rather than as an unsecured claim on another bank — all of that is decided in a different building, under a different process, by the Board of Governors and the Reserve Banks.

For most trust banks that gap is academic. A firm administering estates and pension assets does not need to settle payments overnight in central bank money. For a firm whose stated business is stablecoin payments, the gap is the whole thing. A stablecoin is only as good as the dollars sitting behind it and the speed with which those dollars can be turned into final settlement. Where those dollars sit, and what happens to them on the worst day, is a Federal Reserve question wearing an OCC costume.

## The correspondent problem does not go away

If a chartered stablecoin bank cannot hold balances at the Fed, it holds them at another bank. That means the reserves behind a payments network sit as a claim on a single commercial institution — exactly the arrangement that has made stablecoin reserve custody fragile in the past, and exactly the arrangement a charter was supposed to professionalise. The difference after chartering is that the concentration now carries a federal imprimatur. A customer reading the word "national" on the letterhead will not naturally ask which correspondent is holding the float, or whether that correspondent is itself concentrated across three or four of these new trust banks at once.

That is the risk of granting charters in advance of a settlement policy: you create institutions whose name implies direct access to the monetary system and whose balance sheet depends on a bilateral relationship nobody has disclosed.

## The case for chartering anyway

The strongest argument against me is sequencing in the other direction. You cannot ask the Federal Reserve to rule on account access for an institution that does not exist. The charter comes first because eligibility comes first; a firm must be a legally chartered depository-type institution before it can meaningfully be assessed for an account. On that reading, the OCC is doing its job and the Fed will do its job when there is something to judge. Supervision in the meantime is better than no supervision — a chartered Rain is examined, a vendor Rain is not.

That is fair, and it is why I am not arguing the application should be refused. But eligibility and policy are different things. The Fed does not need a live applicant to say how its account-access framework applies to a class of business whose risk profile is settlement velocity rather than credit. It needs only to write it down. Publishing the test does not pre-judge any firm; withholding it does pre-judge all of them, by forcing each to spend a year and a legal budget on a charter whose commercial value rests on an unstated second decision.

There is also a legal cloud. A trade group has reportedly sued the Comptroller over this very use of trust charters. Granting charters into a contested statutory basis, with the settlement question still blank, is how you end up with institutions holding real customer money while two agencies and a court work out what they are.

## The quiet part at the Fed

The timing makes this sharper. The Fed's own vice chair for supervision has just reorganised the supervisory structure in the name of accountability and clear decision-making authority. Clear decision-making authority is precisely what is missing here. Someone at the Board can say, in a paragraph, whether a national trust bank whose principal activity is stablecoin settlement is the kind of institution that holds reserves at the central bank, and on what conditions. Until someone does, every application filed at the OCC is a bet on an answer nobody has given.
