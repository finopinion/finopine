## The borrowers whose payments rise are the ones who were ahead

That is the part worth sitting with. Under Suncorp, a borrower who had paid extra into their home loan and had money sitting in redraw — the pot of your own overpayments that you can pull back out — could have their minimum monthly repayment reduced. ANZ, which bought Suncorp Bank in 2024 and is now shifting the loan book onto its own systems, does not run that policy. When the loan moves, the minimum reverts to the full contractual amount. One customer told the ABC that Suncorp's email indicated an increase of more than a thousand dollars a month.

Nothing has been taken. The money is still in redraw and the loan balance has not changed. What changed is the obligation. For a household budgeted to the dollar, the compulsory monthly outgoing is the number that matters — it is the one that determines whether the direct debit clears.

## What actually changed

Suncorp wrote to customers on 13 October saying that borrowers with advance payments and available funds in redraw were likely to see their minimum repayment increase at ANZ, and that the increase "could be significant" depending on balance, how far ahead they were and the remaining term. Hundreds of thousands of borrowers are being migrated, with more than a million customers in total due to move by 2027.

None of this was negotiated with the borrower. They chose Suncorp. Suncorp's owner chose ANZ. The feature set is now ANZ's. That sequence is lawful and routine, and it is also the whole problem.

## A price rise that no comparison table records

Mortgage competition in this country is measured on the advertised rate and a short list of fees. Everything in the infrastructure — comparison sites, broker panels, published rate series — is built around that. A feature like repayment recalculation is worth real money to the household using it, and it appears in none of those measurements. So it can be removed in an integration and no index anywhere registers that anything got worse.

That is not a loophole; it is a measurement failure, and it has a direction. Harmonisation is where acquisition value lives. Running two core banking platforms is genuinely expensive, and the cost case for buying a bank of this size is substantially a case about retiring one of them. Australian banking is deep in that phase — thousands of roles out of ANZ over the past year, NAB flagging up to 2,500 this week. Fine. But where part of the saving is delivered by deleting a product feature customers were using, that is a transfer from borrowers to the acquirer, and it should be named as one rather than filed under systems alignment.

## The strongest case for ANZ

It is better than critics allow. Letting the minimum repayment fall because you got ahead quietly undoes the benefit of getting ahead: the debt sits there longer and accrues more interest. ANZ's rule is the stricter and more conventional one, and the borrower who keeps paying the original amount ends up better off. Running two loan platforms indefinitely is an operational risk in its own right — two sets of controls, two hardship processes, two places for errors to go unnoticed — and supervisors have good reason to prefer one. And the bank wrote to customers in advance rather than letting them find out from a failed payment.

All of that is true, and none of it answers the complaint. The question is not whether ANZ's policy is a defensible one to sell. It is whether someone who bought one product can be moved onto a worse-for-them product on the acquirer's timetable, with refinancing as the only remedy. Prudence imposed by surprise on households with no cashflow slack is not prudence — it is an arrears risk with a moral gloss. And the people affected are, by any sensible test, the careful ones.

## The exit that isn't one

The standard answer is: don't like it, refinance. Switching a mortgage means weeks of document gathering, a fresh assessment of whether you can service the loan — tested not at today's rate but at a buffer above it — discharge paperwork and title registration. A borrower about to lose a thousand dollars a month of budget flexibility is precisely the borrower who may now struggle to pass that test somewhere else. The option to leave is weakest at the exact moment it is needed. When consumer advocates say Australian borrowers have little power, this is the mechanism they are describing: the theoretical right to walk, suspended by the thing you are walking away from.

## What a fix looks like

Narrow version: transferred borrowers who were in advance should be able to have their loan re-amortised over a longer term, on request, at no fee and with no new credit assessment — restoring the cashflow position they had, without pretending the feature survived. Discharge fees on transferred loans should be waived for twelve months.

Broad version: a bank acquiring a consumer loan book should have to publish, before completion, a schedule of product features that will not survive integration. Not a warning letter two years later. A document, in advance, that the people approving the deal can read.
