A tax break comes with a number beside it. A waived rule does not.

That single asymmetry explains why an exemption letting cruise ships operate around Australia outside Australian labour law has survived for decades, quietly renewed, while concessions costing a fraction as much get picked over in every budget cycle. On the weekend the federal government extended that exemption again - it was due to lapse at the end of this year - and attached new conditions: crew can bargain collectively and lodge complaints with the maritime safety regulator. The new expiry is 2028.

The timing is not a mystery. In August, an ABC investigation reported crew on some Carnival vessels alleging twelve-hour days seven days a week, medical neglect, drinking water they said was unsafe, and pay below three dollars an hour. Carnival denied the allegations. The Transport Minister called them horrifying. The union says exploitation will now become embedded. What the government actually did was extend the arrangement and bolt improvements onto it.

## A subsidy paid by someone other than the taxpayer

Strip the labour-rights framing away and look at the money. A minimum wage is a price floor. Suspend the floor for one class of operator and the difference between what the law would otherwise require and what is actually paid does not evaporate. It shows up somewhere - in ticket prices, in operating margin, in whether an Australian port call pencils out at all. That is industry assistance. It has a value, a beneficiary and a payer.

The difference from every other form of assistance is the financing. No appropriation is drawn, so no line appears in the budget papers, so no official is ever required to estimate the amount, publish it, and defend it against the next claim on public money. Concessions delivered through the tax system at least get listed and costed, year after year, as revenue forgone. Concessions delivered by switching off a rule get nothing. They are the cheapest thing a government can hand out, because the accounting system cannot see them.

It is also a competitive distortion, and that part should trouble anyone who cares about how markets are structured. Any operator carrying passengers under Australian conditions is competing against one that need not. So is every shore-based tourism business bidding for the same holiday dollar with a cost base set in Australia. Regional economies fight hard for visitor spending - the loss of a single motorsport weekend is being described this week on Phillip Island as an eighty-million-dollar hole - and they do it against a floating competitor whose labour costs are set in an entirely different market.

## Sunsets that only ever move

A sunset clause exists to force a decision. The test of whether one is working is simple: has the thing ever been allowed to lapse, or has the expiry date only ever moved forward? On the evidence here, the date moves. A decades-old exemption reached its scheduled end, and the end was pushed to 2028. That is not a review. It is a renewal with better press attached.

I am not arguing the conditions are worthless. I am arguing they were added at the point of maximum embarrassment rather than at the point of scheduled scrutiny, which tells you the scheduled scrutiny was not doing the work.

## The complaint goes to the wrong door

The enforcement design is the weakest part. A maritime safety regulator exists to oversee vessels, crewing and safe operation. It is not built, funded or staffed to recover unpaid wages, and wage recovery is the specific harm alleged. Routing pay grievances through a safety agency is like sending a billing dispute to the fire brigade - the inspectors may be excellent and still have no remedy to give you.

Collective bargaining has the same problem one layer down. Bargaining power is a function of the outside option, and a seafarer whose job, bed, meals and ability to remain in the country all sit on the same vessel, on a contract measured in months, has very little of one. A right to bargain without a floor beneath the bargaining is worth whatever leverage the weaker party happens to have. Here that is close to none.

## The case for keeping it

The strongest version of the other side is real. Shipping is the most mobile regulatory base there is. An itinerary can be redrawn to skip Australian domestic legs, and the shore spending that disappears falls hardest on regional ports least able to replace it. Australia setting the wages of foreign crew on a foreign-flagged vessel is an extraterritorial reach other flag states may well contest. The Minister's own framing - that the conditions help draw tourists as well as protect workers - is that tourism argument in polite form.

All of it may be true. None of it is an argument against counting the cost. If Australia is buying port calls with a suspended wage floor, the public is entitled to know the price and who is paying it. And the conditions just announced quietly undercut the harder version of the mobility story: if adding bargaining rights does not send the ships away, the constraint is softer than claimed. The question was never whether a floor exists. It is where it sits, and who gets told.
