## The town exists because a rule said it had to

Strip the story back and that is the whole of it. A coal mine was permitted to operate on terms that included housing its workforce in a town nearby rather than flying them in and out. A settlement grew into the space that requirement created. Now the state government has introduced legislation to repeal the requirement, and residents have reached for the phrase that always arrives at this point: ghost town.

The instinct is to treat this as a regional story about a few hundred people and a long drive. It is not. It is a story about whether conditions attached to approvals are promises or merely preferences, and that question reaches every licence, every consent and every undertaking a government extracts from a company in exchange for permission to do something profitable.

## A condition is a price, not a decoration

When a government grants a mining approval it hands over something irreversible and enormously valuable: the right to extract a finite resource on public terms. What it receives in return is a bundle of ongoing obligations. Rehabilitation bonds, royalty arrangements, environmental limits, and sometimes — as here — a requirement about where the workforce lives.

Those obligations are the price. The company weighed them when it bid, financed and built. The state weighed them when it decided the approval was in the public interest. A town then organised itself around the arrangement, and the value of a house in that town is, in part, a claim on the condition continuing to exist.

Repeal by statute cancels the obligation while leaving the approval untouched. The company keeps what it received. The public gives up part of what it was promised. Whatever else that is, it is not deregulation — it is a transfer, with the amount left blank.

## Why the route chosen tells you something

There is normally a process for changing a condition. You apply. The regulator assesses. Reasons are published. Objectors are heard. The outcome can be tested in court. That process is slow and irritating and it exists precisely because the decision affects people who are not party to the negotiation.

Legislation skips all of it. Parliament is entitled to do that; sovereignty is not in doubt. But the choice of instrument is informative. A condition that could plainly survive an assessment does not need a bill to be got rid of. Statute is the route you take when you do not want the assessment.

The same week's news from other states shows what the assessed route looks like when it works. In central New South Wales, approval for a silver mine was overturned in a legal challenge brought by a landcare group and sent back to an independent commission for reassessment. In Queensland, proposed laws would restrict who may object to a mine and give politicians greater control over whether objections are heard in court at all. Different jurisdictions, same direction of travel: the forums where conditions get tested are being narrowed. A repeal by statute is the end point of that logic, not an aberration from it.

## The strongest case for repealing it

And the case is real. Requirements that dictate where a workforce must live are blunt. They raise labour costs, restrict where workers can choose to live and can make a marginal operation uneconomic — in which case the town loses the mine as well as the condition, and sooner. A condition set in one decade may be a poor fit for the labour market of the next. Keeping a settlement alive by compelling people to reside in it is a subsidy in kind, paid by the workforce, that no one ever voted on. And parliament, not a planning officer, is the proper place to correct an obsolete rule.

Every line of that argues for removing the condition on terms. None of it argues for deleting it.

Delay has value. A condition that winds down over five years on a published schedule is a fundamentally different instrument from one that ceases on royal assent; the first gives a town time to convert itself into something that does not depend on the mine, the second does not. Obsolescence is a claim that can be quantified: if the requirement costs more than it delivers, the government can say by how much. The sovereignty argument proves too much, because the same parliament could repeal the approval itself, and nobody thinks it should.

## What would make this legitimate

Three things, none of them exotic. A published costing of the condition — what it costs the operator, what its removal takes from the town. A commencement schedule with dates, so the obligation tapers rather than vanishes. And a requirement that the repeal takes effect only once the operator has entered an enforceable agreement with the local authority covering whatever is being handed back.

Do those and the bill becomes a renegotiation, which is defensible. Skip them and the message to every future proponent is that the conditions written into an approval are the opening position, and the real negotiation happens afterwards, in a chamber where the town does not get a seat.
