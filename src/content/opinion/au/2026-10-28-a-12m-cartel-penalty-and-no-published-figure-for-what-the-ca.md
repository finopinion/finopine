## Twelve million for broccoli

Perfection Fresh Australia, which the ABC describes as the country's second-largest fresh produce supplier, has admitted to the competition regulator that it engaged in cartel conduct on eleven occasions in 2019 and 2020 — fixing the price of broccoli, cauliflower and iceberg lettuce sold to Aldi stores in New South Wales. It has agreed to pay $12 million, subject to the Federal Court approving the figure at a later date.

Twelve million dollars of what, though? Against a grower's margin on vegetables, it sounds enormous. Against the value of the produce sold into a national supermarket chain over two years, it might be rounding. Nobody reading the announcement can tell, because the second number — how much business the conduct touched, and what the regulator thinks it earned — has not been published.

## Deterrence is a sum, and we are shown one side of it

A cartel is a commercial decision. A supplier facing a buyer with real bargaining power asks whether coordinating with rivals pays better than competing, and the answer turns on the gain, the chance of detection, and the cost if detected. Price fixing in fresh produce is detected rarely and slowly: the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission began civil proceedings in September 2025 over conduct it alleges ran from 2018 to 2024 across three states. On a conservative view that detection is far from certain, a penalty has to be a multiple of the gain before it changes anybody's sum.

So the only interesting question about $12 million is what it is a multiple of. The ACCC knows roughly — it has the sales data, it has admissions, and it negotiated the figure. It has chosen to publish the penalty and not the denominator. That turns an enforcement outcome into a press line. Rival suppliers watching this case cannot run the sum the regulator wants them to run.

## What a reasoned penalty looks like

The contrast arrived the same week. In the Federal Court, Justice Cameron Moore fined The Star Entertainment Group $260 million for breaches of anti-money-laundering law, rejecting both the $400 million the financial intelligence agency sought and the $100 million the company argued for, and explaining that he weighed the need to deter a business from putting profit ahead of compliance against the company's financial position. You can disagree with that number. You can see how it was reached.

An agreed penalty inverts the sequence. The parties produce a figure, the court is asked to approve it, and the reasoning — if there is any public reasoning — arrives months later in a judgment most people will never read. In the meantime the figure circulates with nothing behind it. The ACCC's own commissioner said cartel conduct "can increase prices, harming consumers and raising the cost of doing business". Can is the honest word. It is also the problem: in a settlement nobody is required to show that it did, or by how much.

## Why the pass-through question is not academic

The direct counterparty here was a supermarket chain, not a shopper. Whether an inflated wholesale price for lettuce reached the checkout depends on how the retailer priced, and that is an empirical question rather than a slogan. It is also exactly the question a household facing annual inflation of 4.3 per cent, with grocery bills part of the story, would want answered before being told the regulator has protected them.

If the ACCC believes consumers were harmed, it should say what it thinks the harm was. If it believes the harm fell on Aldi and the harm to consumers is unquantified, it should say that too. Enforcement that relies on the word "can" is asking for credit it has not shown.

## The case against this argument

The strongest defence of the settlement is practical. Agreed penalties are how a regulator with a finite litigation budget resolves multi-party cartel cases at all. Perfection Fresh is the first of four supplier groups to admit anything; the proceedings against Hydro Produce, the Velisha companies and M Fragapane & Sons continue, and an early admission plus cooperation is worth a discount precisely because it strengthens the remaining cases. Demanding a published estimate of the overcharge hands every future defendant a number to litigate, and quantifying a cartel gain means constructing a counterfactual price that nobody observed. Settlements could get slower and rarer.

That argument justifies the discount. It does not justify hiding it. Counterfactual pricing estimates are routine in competition damages work, and the ACCC does not need a courtroom-proof figure — it needs a range, a method, and a sentence saying what credit was given for admitting and cooperating. Where the evidence genuinely will not support an estimate, saying so is informative in itself. A discount nobody can see is not an incentive for the next firm to confess; it is a rumour.

And the efficiency case cuts the other way. The whole value of a cheap, fast settlement is the signal it sends to firms that were not sued. A signal with no scale attached sends nothing.
