## The one fee ordinary people can see

A card surcharge is the only line on an Australian receipt that tells the buyer what it costs to move money. Every other payment cost in the economy is wholesale — negotiated between banks, networks and terminal providers, invisible to the person tapping. From 1 October that one visible line disappears from small business counters. The cost behind it does not.

Here is the mechanism, because it is simple and it is being skipped. When a customer taps, the shop pays a fee on the transaction. Some goes to the card network, some to the bank that issued the card, some to whoever supplied the terminal. The shop can absorb that fee out of margin, or pass it on. A surcharge is a pass-through that is separated out and labelled. Building it into prices is also a pass-through — just an unlabelled one, spread across every sale, including sales paid for in cash.

So the rule taking effect next month does not decide *whether* customers pay for card acceptance. It decides only whether they can see it, and whether the people who use the cheapest payment methods subsidise the people who use the dearest.

## Take away the till, and the expensive card wins

The deeper problem is what the ban does to demand. A surcharge is a crude but real piece of discipline: if tapping a premium credit card costs the buyer something extra and tapping debit does not, some buyers switch. Volume moves toward the cheaper rail. That movement is the only pressure on card pricing that originates with the customer rather than with a regulator.

After 1 October, at the point of sale, every card is free. Nothing at the counter distinguishes the cheapest instrument from the most expensive one to accept. The predictable result is that expensive cards gain share, which pushes the *average* cost of acceptance up for merchants — at exactly the moment the merchant loses the ability to say so on the docket. A rule sold as relief for small business can raise the bill it was meant to reduce, and hide the increase.

This lands unevenly. A national chain negotiates on volume, absorbs the fee and changes nothing a customer notices. A café in a regional town has to reprice its menu, and when it does, the price rise reads as greed rather than as a cost imposed from somewhere it has never had a say. That is the trap in the operator's phrase quoted in the reporting: cost creep is insidious precisely because each increment arrives as a fraction of a per cent per transaction, too small to trigger a renegotiation, large enough in aggregate to close a business.

## Two arms of policy, pulling opposite ways

Days before all this, the Reserve Bank of Australia designated Linfox Armaguard under the Cash Distribution Framework Act 2026 — a statutory framework built because moving physical cash around the country no longer supports itself commercially. Cash is the payment method with no acceptance fee attached.

So the state is legislating to keep cash circulating while simultaneously removing the price signal that would send customers back to it. You can defend either policy on its own. Defending both at once requires an explanation nobody has offered.

## The strongest case for the ban

It is this: surcharging as actually practised was frequently a lie. Flat percentages applied to every transaction regardless of what the underlying card cost. The cheapest debit tap charged as if it were a premium credit card. Amounts that exceeded the merchant's real cost of acceptance and quietly became a margin line. On top of that, drip pricing corrodes the meaning of an advertised price, and a customer standing at a terminal with a queue behind them is not in a negotiating position. "Choice" at the counter is partly a fiction. And a rule you can enforce — no surcharges — beats a rule you cannot police, which is what "surcharge no more than your cost" turned out to be.

That case is strong, and it concedes the point. The complaint was that the signal was inaccurate, not that it was uninformative. Replacing an inaccurate signal with no signal only helps if something else takes over the job of holding fees down. There is exactly one candidate: a binding cap on what merchants are charged, tightly set and continuously enforced. If that cap exists and bites, the surcharge was redundant and the ban costs nothing. If it does not, then from October the entire payments cost stack sits inside consumer prices where no constituency will ever notice it rising again.

That is the test, and it is not rhetorical. It is arithmetic that will be observable within a year — provided somebody publishes it.

## What would settle it

Not anecdote, and not the volume of complaints, which will fade as the fee vanishes into menu prices. The settling evidence is a single series: what merchants in the smallest turnover band actually pay per dollar accepted, tracked from before the ban to well after it. If that number falls, the reform worked. If it drifts up while retail prices absorb it silently, the reform moved money and called it protection.
