## The ceiling is not the target

The Reserve Bank of Australia lifted the cash rate to 4.85 per cent on Tuesday on the strength of a forecast in which inflation never actually comes back to target.

Read the sentence that does the work. Inflation "is not expected to reach the top of the 2–3 per cent target range until late 2027, around three quarters later than previously forecast." The top. Not the middle. The Board measured its own slippage — three quarters of it — against a number it does not aim at.

Six days before the meeting, Governor Michele Bullock told a Senate estimates committee that the Monetary Policy Board "sets monetary policy such that inflation is expected to return to the midpoint of the 2–3 per cent target range." There is flexibility on timing, she said, to balance that against full employment. But the objective is 2.5 per cent, not 3.

The decision statement never bridges the two. It tells you when inflation is forecast to scrape into the band and stops. The gap between 3 and 2.5 is half the band. In time, it is unknowable from this document, because the document does not say.

## The expectations problem the Board created for itself

This would be a drafting quibble if the Board's case for tightening rested on something else. It does not. The statement says the later return to the band "presents upside risks to inflation expectations; some measures are assessed to have risen lately." Bullock put the logic plainly to the Senate: inflation must not diverge from target for too long, because that risks it becoming embedded in expectations, which then feeds into price and wage setting.

Now apply that logic to the published path. By Bullock's own account, inflation picked up in the second half of 2025 and has run around 3.5 to 4 per cent since. The new forecast adds roughly two more years before inflation touches the ceiling — and says nothing about when it reaches the middle. That is the best part of four years away from target on the Board's own numbers, with the midpoint date left blank.

You cannot invoke expectations drift as the reason to raise rates and then publish a destination that gives expectations every reason to keep drifting. One of those two things has to give. The honest reading is that the Board expects to do more than this statement concedes.

## Who the lever reaches

There is a second problem buried in the same page. The statement is explicit that higher rates and falling housing prices will weigh on dwelling investment and household consumption. That is the transmission channel: mortgaged households and homebuilding.

But it also forecasts stronger near-term business investment "including data centre-related investment," and says artificial-intelligence-related investment is driving rapid growth in prices for technology-related goods. That spending is not funded out of Australian household balance sheets and does not respond much to an Australian cash rate. So one source of capacity pressure is largely outside the instrument's reach, which means the instrument has to press harder on everything it can reach to get the same result.

That does not make the rise wrong. It makes the burden narrower and larger than the headline implies, and the Board should say so in its own words rather than leave it to be inferred.

## The case against this argument

The strongest defence is that flexible inflation targeting is supposed to look like this. Much of the shock is a supply shock — the statement attributes higher fuel and commodity prices to the conflict in the Middle East. Unemployment has risen and is projected to rise by more than forecast in August. Housing prices have fallen significantly. Forcing inflation to 2.5 per cent quickly would cost jobs for no durable gain, and a long return path is the deliberate, defensible choice.

Three of the nine members agreed enough to vote against the rise. They have a respectable case: 125 basis points of tightening this year has not fully passed through, and markets in late October were barely pricing a November move at all. And forecasts stretching to late 2027 are soft enough that arguing about the quarter in which they cross a line is close to astrology.

All of that is fair. None of it justifies describing the destination as the top of the band.

If the Board is choosing a slower return in order to protect employment, that is a legitimate judgement and it should be written down as a judgement — which objective is being traded, by how much, and for how long. The statement does not do that. The reasons it gives for the slower return are higher fuel prices and tighter domestic capacity. Those are reasons the return is harder, not reasons the Board chose patience. A reader cannot tell the two apart from this document, and the difference is the whole of monetary policy.

As for forecast uncertainty: the statement itself lists scenarios in which inflation is higher and activity lower. Uncertainty is not a reason to publish a vaguer target. It is a reason to publish a clearer one.
