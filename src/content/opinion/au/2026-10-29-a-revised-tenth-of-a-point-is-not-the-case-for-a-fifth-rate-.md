## A tenth of a point, measured from a number that moved

Underlying inflation in Australia rose by one tenth of a percentage point in September — from a figure that had been revised downwards after it was first published. Within hours of the release, market pricing for a rate rise at next Tuesday's Reserve Bank meeting sat at roughly 85 per cent.

The Australian Bureau of Statistics reported that consumer prices rose 0.8 per cent in the month, against 0.4 per cent in August, taking the annual rate to 4.3 per cent from 4.0. Housing was the largest single contributor at 5.2 per cent over the year. Transport did most of the rest of the work, driven by fuel, which was up 9.4 per cent.

That is the headline. The number the Reserve Bank watches more closely is the trimmed mean — a measure that throws away the items with the biggest price rises and the biggest price falls each period, on the theory that what is left is the underlying trend rather than one-off shocks. The trimmed mean came in at 3.6 per cent in the year to September. The August reading, revised, was 3.5.

So the shock in the headline came substantially from fuel, which the trimmed mean excludes precisely because it is volatile. And the core measure rose by a single tenth, against a base that had moved after the fact. One economist called the release "the last data point necessary" for a fifth increase this year. On the underlying measure, it was barely a data point at all.

## What the Governor actually said

On the same day, the Reserve Bank Governor gave an opening statement to a Senate committee that made the case for tightening without needing September at all. Inflation fell from a peak of 7.9 per cent at the end of 2022, sat inside the 2–3 per cent target band for a period across 2024 and 2025, then picked up again over the second half of 2025 and has stayed above target since. Recently it has run around three and a half to four per cent in year-ended terms. The Board has raised the cash rate by a cumulative 100 basis points this year. And the stated worry is that inflation which diverges from target for too long risks becoming embedded in expectations, which then feeds into price and wage setting.

That is a coherent argument for moving again. It is an argument about duration, not about September. Four rate rises have not pulled inflation back to the band, and the longer the gap persists the harder it is to close. Nothing in it depends on whether the trimmed mean printed 3.5 or 3.6.

## Why the difference matters

It matters because markets price the reaction function, not the sentiment. If the understanding is that a monthly indicator decides the meeting, then every monthly release becomes a binary event — and the series that decides it is one the statistician openly revises, as the August figure demonstrates. That is an unstable arrangement. It imports measurement noise straight into the yield curve and, through it, into what borrowers and savers pay.

It also blurs which number the Bank is actually targeting. If a fuel-driven headline print is what shifts the balance, because fuel prices are what households see and therefore what moves expectations, that is a legitimate position — but it is a different reaction function from one anchored on a core measure that excludes fuel by design. Market participants are entitled to know which they are pricing.

## The strongest case against this

The best counter is that central banks never get clean data and cannot wait for it. Monthly figures are the timeliest read available; insisting on the quarterly means being a quarter late to a problem that compounds. Revisions run in both directions, and the August revision went down, which if anything means the Bank had been looking at an overstated figure and the underlying picture deteriorated more than it seemed. Housing at 5.2 per cent is not a statistical artefact. And a headline at 4.3 per cent, whatever drives it, is the number households and wage negotiators see.

Most of that is right, and it argues for hiking. It does not argue for presenting one month as the trigger. If the justification is that inflation has been stuck near four per cent through a hundred basis points of tightening, say that — it is stronger, it is robust to revision, and it does not collapse if October comes in soft.

## What would settle it

The gap here is observable and closeable. Nobody outside the Bank knows how much weight the monthly indicator carries relative to the quarterly, and nobody outside the statistical agency knows how much the monthly trimmed mean typically moves between its first and final estimates. Both of those are publishable facts. Until they are published, a market pricing near-certainty off a tenth of a percentage point is behaving rationally given what it has been told — which is the problem.
