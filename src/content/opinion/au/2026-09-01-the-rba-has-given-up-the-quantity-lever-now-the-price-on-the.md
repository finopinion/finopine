## The word doing the work is not "ample"

"Ample" is a word about quantity. The regime the Reserve Bank of Australia has named after it will be settled by a price.

On 25 August the Bank's Head of Domestic Markets, David Jacobs, set out the path in a speech called "The Road to Ample — Towards a Demand-driven Liquidity Regime". Both halves of that title are terms of art, and they point in slightly different directions. "Ample" is the destination: a banking system holding comfortably more settlement balances — the money banks keep at the central bank in order to pay each other — than it strictly needs to get through a normal day. "Demand-driven" is the method. The Reserve Bank does not decide how many of those balances exist. It posts a price and a list of acceptable collateral, and lends whatever quantity the banks come and ask for.

That is a genuine surrender of control, and a deliberate one. The alternative is a supply-driven system, in which the central bank estimates the system's appetite for reserves and buys assets until it thinks it has satisfied it. Guess too high and you carry a balance sheet you did not need. Guess too low and you starve the payment system on a Tuesday afternoon. Demand-driven removes the guess. But it swaps one hard problem for another: having handed the quantity lever to the banks, the Bank keeps only the price lever, and everything now turns on where that price sits.

## Why the spread is the entire design

Banks will obtain reserves mainly through repurchase agreements — selling securities to the Reserve Bank and agreeing to buy them back a short time later. The rate on those operations becomes the marginal cost of settlement liquidity for the whole system. Nothing else in the framework competes with it.

Set that rate at or barely above the cash rate target — the overnight interest rate the Reserve Bank Board actually sets — and no bank has any reason to sit on a shortfall. The observed cash rate is welded to the target, because a cheap alternative is available on demand to anyone with collateral. Set it meaningfully above the target and you have invited every bank treasurer in the country to economise: hold a fatter precautionary buffer, hesitate at the margin, arrive at the window last rather than first. The quantity of reserves in the system is then not "ample" in any meaningful sense. It is whatever reluctance permits.

This is the part of the transition that deserves argument, and it is the part most easily lost in the language of plumbing. A demand-driven regime does not guarantee ample reserves. It guarantees only that reserves will be as ample as banks are willing to ask for at the posted price.

## Stigma is a design flaw, not a temperament

The standard objection to relying on banks to come forward is stigma — the worry that borrowing from the central bank marks a firm as troubled. Stigma is usually discussed as if it were a psychological quirk that education might cure. It is not. It is a rational response to a price signal. If the window costs more than the market, using it says something about the user, because a solvent bank with options would not pay the premium. The premium creates the inference. Remove the premium and the inference dissolves, because everyone uses the facility all the time and using it says nothing at all.

That is why the design and the culture cannot be separated. A regime that wants routine, unremarkable borrowing has to price borrowing so it is routine and unremarkable. Frequent operations, a broad collateral list, pre-positioned collateral tested in advance rather than in an emergency, and a spread small enough that no board paper is required to justify tapping it.

## The strongest case for making it expensive

The case against me is serious. A window priced at the target makes the central bank the cheapest lender in the country. The interbank market — banks lending spare balances to each other overnight — withers, because there is no reason to shop around. With it goes a price signal and a form of peer discipline: banks that other banks are wary of lending to used to find that out early and cheaply. And a cheap, permanent facility encourages institutions to hold thinner buffers of liquid assets, knowing the central bank will fill the gap.

I think that argument fails on its own terms. An interbank market that exists only because the alternative has been deliberately overpriced is not price discovery. It is a toll, and what banks are paying is not a price for credit risk — central bank repo is collateralised — but a fee for administrative discouragement. Nor should the operating framework be doing the supervisor's job. If banks are holding too few liquid assets, that is a matter for liquidity rules, capital rules and supervision. Using the settlement system's pricing as a substitute for prudential regulation gets you weak versions of both: a rate target that misses at quarter-ends, and risk-taking that the rules were supposed to constrain anyway.

There is a further Australian reason to go cheap. The Payments System Board is currently consulting on the role of the Reserve Bank Information and Transfer System — the country's high-value settlement system — in supporting settlement in a tokenised ecosystem, and on the future of account-to-account payments. Both point toward liquidity needs that are faster, lumpier and harder to forecast. A regime that expects banks to pre-empt their own shortfalls, at a penalty, is exactly the wrong regime for that world.
