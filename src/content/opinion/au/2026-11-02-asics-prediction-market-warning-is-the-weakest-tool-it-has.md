## Same cashflow, two legal worlds

A contract that pays you one dollar if a named candidate wins and nothing if they lose is, in cash terms, identical to a bet on the same outcome at the same odds. The money moves the same way. What differs is which rulebook applies — and that decides whether the person on the losing end has a disclosure document, money held separately from the operator's own, an external complaints body, and a licensee that can be sued or stripped of its authorisation.

The Australian Securities and Investments Commission, which licenses financial firms and markets in this country, has now said it is not currently convinced that prediction markets for retail clients are in the public interest. It paired that with a warning: Australians who go to offshore platforms have no legal protections. Both halves are true. Read together, they describe a problem the regulator has named without solving.

## A posture is not a rule

"Not currently convinced" is a statement about how an application would be received, not a statement about what the law is. It is an extremely efficient instrument. Firms read it and do not apply. Nothing is refused on the record. Nothing is appealed. No criteria are published, so no operator can design to them and no critic can argue they are wrong. Regulation by discouragement costs the regulator almost nothing and produces no precedent at all.

That is fine when the activity in question requires Australian infrastructure. It is close to useless when the activity requires a browser and a funding method. The very sentence warning about offshore platforms tells you the discouragement is working only on the firms that would have submitted to Australian rules — which is to say, the ones you would most want inside the tent.

## The question being avoided

"Public interest" is a single switch. It collapses a list of specific, answerable questions into one yes-or-no that can be left unresolved indefinitely. The specific questions are not mysterious. Who holds customer money, and is it separated from the operator's own balance sheet? Who determines the outcome, from what source, and what happens when that source is ambiguous or revised? Are there position limits? Is there age verification? Is the operator a member of the external dispute resolution scheme that binds other licensed financial firms? What may be advertised, where, and to whom?

Every one of those is a licence condition. None of them is settled by declaring the whole category unpersuasive. And the category itself is not uniform: a contract on next quarter's inflation print and a contract on a celebrity's marriage are not the same product in any sense a regulator should care about, even if they share a venue.

## The case against this argument

The strongest objection is that these are gambling products wearing a brokerage costume, and that licensing them would confer exactly the legitimacy their promoters want. Gambling in Australia is regulated by the states, with harm-minimisation rules, advertising restrictions and self-exclusion schemes attached. Financial services regulation assumes a client who is investing or hedging, not one who is chasing losses. Hand event contracts a financial services licence and you let an operator trade up into the lighter-touch regime by changing the noun.

There is a second, better objection: friction is itself a protection. An offshore platform that cannot take an Australian bank transfer has to be funded some harder way, and that difficulty keeps the volumes small. A licensed domestic venue with instant funding would not be smaller than the offshore market — it would be many times larger. Prohibition by inconvenience may be inelegant, but it is doing real work.

Both objections are arguments about which rulebook, and how strict. Neither is an argument for leaving the question open. If event contracts on social and sporting outcomes are wagering, say so plainly and send them to the gambling regulators, with the harm rules that come with that classification. If contracts on economic data are derivatives, licence them with conditions and keep the product intervention power — the tool that lets the regulator restrict how a product is sold to retail clients — loaded for when the conditions prove inadequate.

And the friction argument cuts the other way too. Friction that pushes customers onto crypto rails is friction that removes the chargeback, removes the traceable counterparty, and removes any realistic prospect of recovering funds if the venue fails or is drained. Making a market hard to reach is not the same as making it safe.

## What is actually at stake

The worst available outcome is the one currently in force: no domestic venue, no domestic obligations, no domestic complaints path, and a population that can reach the product in two taps. A consumer alert does not change the economics of that. It transfers responsibility to the consumer and leaves the perimeter exactly where it was.

Australia has shown it can do better than this when it chooses to — it has faced down leveraged retail derivatives by letting them exist and then regulating how they are sold, rather than by hoping the demand went away. The same approach is available here. What is missing is a decision.
