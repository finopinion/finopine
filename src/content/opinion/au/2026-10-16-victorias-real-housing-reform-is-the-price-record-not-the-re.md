## The number nobody has to tell you

The figure that matters most in a house purchase is the one that has been hardest to obtain: what the place down the road actually sold for. Everything else in the transaction is an estimate built on top of it. From this month, in Victoria, agents must publish that number and keep it published for at least 18 months.

That is the half of the new law that got the smaller headline, and it is the half that will still be doing useful work in five years. The other half — publishing the seller's reserve price a week before the auction — produced the minister's soundbite about wasted weekends and the industry's objection that reserves move. It is also the half most likely to be litigated, amended or quietly watered down.

## Underquoting is an asymmetry, not a character flaw

Underquoting means advertising a property at a price well below what it is expected to fetch, so that more people turn up and bid. The cost lands on buyers who were never in contention: building inspections, conveyancing advice, finance applications, and the weekends the Victorian consumer affairs minister referred to. None of that is refundable when the hammer falls two hundred thousand dollars above the quoted range.

The reason the practice has been so hard to stamp out is structural. A quoted price range is supposed to be grounded in comparable sales. The agent has the comparables. The buyer, in the main, does not. Enforcement therefore means a regulator reconstructing, after the fact, what one agent knew about one property — expensive, slow, and necessarily rare.

A public register of outcomes flips that. If every sale price is on the record for a year and a half, any buyer can test a quoted range against the street before spending a cent on a building report. Thousands of people checking cheaply beats one enforcement team checking expensively. It is the only form of consumer protection in housing that scales.

## The fragile half

A reserve is the lowest price a seller will accept. Publishing it a week out is a bigger change to the auction than it sounds: a sale with a secret floor is a game in which neither side knows where the floor sits, and publishing it turns the floor into an anchor that shapes the opening bid and the finish.

It also runs into a dilemma the government has not obviously resolved. Either the published reserve binds the vendor on the day, in which case the Real Estate Institute of Victoria's complaint has force — markets and bidding genuinely do move what a seller will accept between Monday and Saturday — or it does not bind, in which case what has been disclosed is an intention, and intentions can be revised upward at five to twelve. Transparency about a number that may be abandoned is not transparency about the price.

The state should say which it is, in terms an ordinary bidder can understand, because the answer determines whether the rule is worth defending at all.

## Why the register matters beyond the auction

Housing finance runs on comparable sales. Lenders' valuers use them. Automated valuation models used in mortgage approvals are built on them. Buyers deciding what to offer, and what deposit they need, are implicitly doing the same arithmetic with worse inputs. When the record of what properties fetched is not required to be published, the quality of anyone's estimate depends on who they can pay for data. Publishing outcomes is therefore not just a consumer-affairs measure; it narrows the gap between the professional estimate and the household one.

## The case against this argument

Two objections deserve answering properly.

The first is the industry's: that the rules are confusing and inconsistent, and that a published reserve may deter bidders who conclude they cannot reach it, thinning auctions and costing vendors — who are ordinary people too, often in a market that has been among the country's weakest. That is a real risk. But a thinner auction of serious bidders is the point. The bidders deterred are, by construction, the ones who were never going to win, and whose attendance was the whole purpose of a misleading quote. And the weakness of the reserve rule is an argument for fixing the reserve rule, not for abandoning the price record alongside it.

The second is privacy: a sale price is a household's financial position made public. But it is a transaction concluded in public, at a public auction, already known to the agent, the buyer, the underbidders and the state revenue office. Making it available to the next buyer in the same street is not an intrusion. If anything, 18 months is the wrong number in the wrong direction. Comparables do not expire on schedule.

## What would settle it

One statistic would resolve the argument about reserves within a year: how often a published reserve was changed on auction day, and in which direction. Nobody currently has to report it. Somebody should.
