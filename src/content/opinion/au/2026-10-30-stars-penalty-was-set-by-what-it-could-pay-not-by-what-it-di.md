## The number came out of the accounts

Star Entertainment told the Federal Court that any penalty above $100 million would push it into liquidation. The financial intelligence agency, AUSTRAC, asked for $400 million. Justice Cameron Moore landed on $260 million, saying he had weighed the need for deterrence against the casino's financial position.

That last phrase is doing an enormous amount of work. Deterrence looks backwards at conduct — in this case, AUSTRAC's allegation that Star failed to manage the money-laundering risks in its dealings with the Asian gambling junket Suncity and its funder, and kept dealing with them after reports of criminal links surfaced. Financial position looks at the defendant today. The two were blended into one figure, and the only part of that figure anyone can check against anything is the part Star supplied: its own survival line.

## Distress is partly the product of the conduct

Capacity to pay is a legitimate sentencing consideration everywhere in the law, and nobody sensible wants courts bankrupting companies for sport. But in corporate financial-crime cases it runs in an awkward direction. The conduct that attracts the penalty is also, frequently, the conduct that wrecks the business. Star's casino licences in New South Wales and Queensland were suspended. A company whose licences have been suspended is, by definition, less able to pay a fine than one whose licences have not.

So the discount tracks the damage, and the damage tracks the conduct. Run a clean book and get fined on your earnings; run a dirty one badly enough to lose your licences, and the court must take your diminished earnings into account. Nobody designed that incentive on purpose. It falls out of a regime whose only real lever is a single lump sum levied on the entity.

## Four years is a discount in itself

AUSTRAC filed in 2022. Judgment came in 2026. The conduct is assessed as of when it happened; the capacity to pay is assessed as of the day the judge rules. A defendant who contests to the end therefore holds an option: if the business deteriorates during the proceedings, the penalty deteriorates with it. If it recovers, it pays what a recovered business can pay. There is no symmetric risk that four years of litigation makes the fine larger.

That asymmetry is the real argument for speed in enforcement. A case resolved in eighteen months is priced against roughly the same company that committed the breaches. A case resolved in four years is priced against whatever is left.

## The case for going easy

The serious objection deserves stating properly. A penalty that tips a company into liquidation collects almost nothing: a civil penalty ranks as an unsecured debt, employees lose jobs, and the casualties are overwhelmingly people with no connection to junket accounts. Deterrence, on this view, was delivered by other instruments — suspended licences, forced changes at the top, remediation spending, and the public ordeal of an annual meeting held the day before judgment. Adding a liquidating fine on top is punishment theatre with a negative expected recovery.

And the court did not simply take dictation. Justice Moore said $100 million was too low. He set the penalty at more than twice the figure Star itself nominated as the edge of survival, which is not the behaviour of a judge accepting a liquidation claim at face value.

Both points are fair. Neither rescues the structure. The problem is not that $260 million is the wrong number — I have no way of knowing, and neither does anyone reading the headline. The problem is that the instrument has only one dial, and a defendant in difficulty can always reach it.

## A fine does not have to be a lump sum

There is an obvious alternative that Australian enforcement has barely used. Split the penalty. Fix one component on the conduct — contraventions, duration, benefit — and make a second component contingent and deferred: payable out of earnings over a defined window, or crystallising if the company resumes distributions to shareholders within some number of years. The firm stays solvent. The deterrent survives. And the incentive to arrive in court as poor as possible disappears, because poverty buys a schedule rather than a reduction.

This is not exotic. Regulators routinely accept enforceable undertakings with multi-year obligations, and courts order payment by instalments. What is missing is a deliberate practice of asking for penalties that scale with recovery, and reasons that separate the conduct-based figure from the affordability adjustment so that outsiders can see which is which.

The second limb matters more over time. Any sanction aimed solely at the entity is capped by the entity's balance sheet. Obligations attached to senior officers are not, and a regime that relies entirely on corporate fines will always find its deterrent weakest against the firms that let things go furthest.

Every bank, remitter, payments business and exchange that has to fund an anti-money-laundering function read this judgment. What they can reasonably take from it is that the tail risk is bounded — not by the gravity of the breach, but by the state of the accounts on the day it is finally judged.
